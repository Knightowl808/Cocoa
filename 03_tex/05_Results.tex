\section{Empirical Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section presents the out-of-sample forecasting results, organized around
the two research questions. Section~\ref{sec:res-mean} compares mean forecast
accuracy across the three baseline models. Section~\ref{sec:res-har-qr}
evaluates distributional forecasts at the upper tail, testing whether HAR-QR
outperforms the benchmarks across horizons (RQ1). Section~\ref{sec:res-har-x-qr}
assesses whether the Ni\~no~3.4 climate index adds forecast value at long
horizons (RQ2). Section~\ref{sec:res-mcs} tests the statistical significance
of the observed differences using Model Confidence Sets.
Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness} subjects the ENSO result to three diagnostics
addressing temporal stability, lag structure, and cross-commodity replication.
Section~\ref{sec:res-summary} synthesizes the findings. All
results use a rolling estimation window of 2,520 trading days with daily
re-estimation. All models are evaluated on a common sample at each horizon,
yielding between 7,306 (twelve months) and 7,737 (one day) out-of-sample
forecast--realization pairs.

The gap between HAR-OLS and GARCH is largest at the one-month horizon
(0.084 QLIKE units) and narrowest at one day (0.029 units). The monthly
HAR component ($\sigma^{(m)}_t$) maintains a correlation of 0.66 with the
twelve-month volatility target, compared to 0.37 for the daily component,
an empirical fact that points to the source of HAR-OLS's long-horizon
advantage.

GARCH(1,1) outperforms historical volatility at every horizon. The margin
is widest at one day (0.106 QLIKE units), where GARCH's daily updating
reacts to volatility shocks that a trailing 252-day average smooths away,
nearly vanishes at one month (0.003 units), and widens again toward twelve
months (0.057 units). All three models share a common horizon pattern:
loss improves from the one-day to the one-month horizon, reflecting the
smoothing effect of averaging in the target $\bar{\sigma}_{t:t+h}$, before
degrading at three through twelve months as the autoregressive signal
weakens. Even HAR-OLS degrades beyond one month, which motivates the
search for additional predictive signals in
Section~\ref{sec:res-har-x-qr}. The mechanism behind the GARCH and HAR
ranking is interpreted in Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq1}.

The QLIKE ranking is broadly confirmed by MSE and MAE.
Figure~\ref{fig:robustness-lineplot} plots all three loss functions across
horizons. The HAR family achieves the lowest MSE and MAE at every horizon,
and from one week onward GARCH(1,1) records the highest error under both
metrics, a direct consequence of mean-reversion in its multi-step point
forecasts. Under QLIKE, by contrast, GARCH ranks second throughout and
stays ahead of historical volatility even at twelve months. The HAR
family's lead over both benchmarks is preserved regardless of how point
forecast errors are penalized.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Quantile Forecast Comparison (RQ1)}
\label{sec:res-har-qr}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Mean forecasts summarize only the center of the conditional distribution.
For risk assessment, the upper tail matters: a model that accurately forecasts
average volatility may still understate the probability of extreme outcomes.
We now compare five models on their ability to forecast the 95th percentile
of future volatility. Historical volatility uses the empirical quantile of
the trailing 252-day distribution of $h$-day average volatility. GARCH(1,1)
quantiles follow the closed-form Gaussian approach
(Equation~\ref{eq:garch-quantile}). HAR-OLS produces pseudo-quantile
forecasts from its in-sample residual distribution
(Equation~\ref{eq:har-ols-quantile}). HAR-QR estimates the quantile directly
through quantile regression on the HAR structure, and HAR-X-QR augments
this with the Ni\~no~3.4 index.

Table~\ref{tab:upper-qlike-comparison} reports the quantile loss at
$\tau = 0.95$ for all five models.

Four patterns emerge from this comparison.

\textit{First, direct quantile estimation wins at short and medium
horizons.} HAR-QR achieves the lowest quantile loss at one day, one week,
one month, and (marginally) three months. The advantage over the
benchmarks is not trivial: at one month, the best benchmark (GARCH,
$0.000609$) is 22\% above HAR-QR ($0.000500$), and at one day historical
volatility ($0.001281$) is 11\% above HAR-QR ($0.001159$). Against the
HAR-OLS pseudo-quantile, HAR-QR improves by 6\% at one day and 7\% at one
month; at three months the two are essentially tied ($0.000557$ vs
$0.000558$).

\textit{Second, the ranking reverses at long horizons.} At six months the
HAR-OLS pseudo-quantile takes the lead at $0.000608$, which is 14\% below
HAR-QR. At twelve months GARCH(1,1) becomes the best model outright, with
a quantile loss ($0.000736$) that is 29\% below HAR-QR and 27\% below
HAR-X-QR. The advantage of
estimating the quantile directly on rolling windows is thus a
short-horizon phenomenon; at the longest horizons, simpler quantile
constructions anchored to the unconditional distribution prove more
robust. Whether these long-horizon reversals are statistically meaningful
is assessed in Section~\ref{sec:res-mcs}.

\textit{Third, historical volatility collapses at long horizons.} Its
empirical trailing quantile is competitive at one month ($0.000691$) but
deteriorates rapidly beyond that, reaching $0.001462$ at twelve months ---
40\% above HAR-QR and 99\% above GARCH. A trailing one-year window cannot
anticipate distributional shifts over a further one-year forecast window,
so violations cluster exactly where the upper tail matters most.

\textit{Fourth, ENSO augmentation does not separate HAR-X-QR from HAR-QR
in this model's favor except at twelve months.} The two models are
virtually indistinguishable at one day and one week (differences
$<0.5\%$); ENSO \emph{worsens} the upper-tail forecast at one month
($-4.2\%$), three months ($-4.2\%$), and six months ($-1.0\%$); and only
at twelve months does a modest improvement of $3.3\%$ appear. This
horizon dependence is analyzed in detail in
Section~\ref{sec:res-har-x-qr}.

Table~\ref{tab:upper-qlike-comparison} isolates the upper tail, where the
long-horizon ranking reverses in favour of the benchmarks. The full
five-model comparison at the median and $\tau = 0.75$, reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary} (Table~\ref{tab:quantile-loss-all}),
shows that this reversal is confined to the upper tail: at $\tau = 0.50$
and $\tau = 0.75$ the HAR family (HAR-QR or HAR-OLS) records the lowest
quantile loss at every horizon, and neither benchmark is ever competitive.
The twelve-month advantage of GARCH is thus a tail phenomenon, not a
general long-horizon one.

Figure~\ref{fig:upper-tail} illustrates the distributional forecasts of
the HAR-X-QR model, displaying the 50--75\% and 75--95\% quantile bands
alongside the median forecast ($\tau = 0.50$) and Yang-Zhang volatility.
Panels (a1) and (b1) cover 1995--2022; panels (a2) and (b2) show the
2023--2025 crisis window on its own scale. The figure complements the
loss-function evidence by showing how the model's forecast distribution
moves through regimes.

At the one-month horizon (panels~a1, a2), the quantile bands widen and
narrow in step with Yang-Zhang volatility, demonstrating the HAR
structure's ability to adapt to changing variance regimes; during the
2024 crisis peak the bands follow the realized spike with a short lag. At
the twelve-month horizon (panels~b1, b2), the bands are smoother,
reflecting the averaging inherent in long-run projection. The crisis
panel (b2) exposes the central weakness of the long-horizon forecast:
Yang-Zhang volatility rises above the $\tau = 0.95$ boundary for an
extended stretch of 2023--24 before the forecast distribution adjusts,
and the bands widen only after the spike has materialized. This delayed
adjustment is reflected in the violation rates reported in
Table~\ref{tab:upper-coverage}.

The dominant feature of this table is the prevalence of negative entries:
in fifteen of the eighteen quantile--horizon combinations, adding the
Ni\~no~3.4 index \emph{worsens} the out-of-sample forecast. At short
horizons (one day to one week) the effect is negligible in either
direction --- the additional coefficient neither helps nor hurts
materially. From one month onward, the extra parameter degrades
performance at almost every quantile level, with the deterioration most
pronounced at $\tau = 0.75$, where it grows monotonically with the
horizon and reaches $-6.7\%$ at twelve months.

The only improvements appear at the twelve-month horizon: $+3.3\%$ at the
upper tail ($\tau = 0.95$) and $+1.6\%$ at the median. This single
favourable cell is the entire empirical case for RQ2, and three
properties place it under suspicion before any further testing. It is
modest in magnitude; it is isolated, surrounded by deterioration at every
shorter horizon; and it earns HAR-X-QR no top rank in the Model
Confidence Set at any horizon (Section~\ref{sec:res-mcs}). It must also
be read against the benchmark comparison in
Table~\ref{tab:upper-qlike-comparison}: even with the ENSO gain, HAR-X-QR
($0.001007$) remains 37\% above GARCH(1,1) ($0.000736$) at the
twelve-month upper tail. The climate index narrows the gap to the best
model; it does not close it. The gain cannot be dismissed on magnitude
alone, however, because it falls precisely at the twelve-month
procurement horizon that motivates the thesis. It therefore warrants a
direct test of whether it reflects a stable predictive relationship or a
sample-specific regime effect --- the subject of
Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness}.

\subsection{Statistical Significance: Model Confidence Sets}
\label{sec:res-mcs}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The preceding sections compare models by point estimates of loss functions.
To assess whether the observed differences are statistically significant,
we apply the Model Confidence Set (MCS) procedure of
\textcite{hansen2011model}. The MCS identifies the set
$\mathcal{M}^{*}$ of models whose forecasting ability cannot be
distinguished from that of the best model at a given confidence level.
We use the MCS procedure and configuration described in
Section~\ref{sec:meth-evaluation}.

Table~\ref{tab:mcs-qlike} reports MCS results for QLIKE loss.
HAR-OLS is the sole member of $\mathcal{M}^{*}$ at the one-day, one-week,
and one-month horizons ($p = 1.00$), confirming that its mean forecast
superiority is statistically significant at short and medium horizons.
From three months onward, all five models enter $\mathcal{M}^{*}$: at
long horizons the loss differentials, while still favoring HAR-OLS in
point terms, are no longer large relative to their sampling variation.
GARCH(1,1) and historical volatility are thus excluded through one month
and statistically indistinguishable from the best model thereafter.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Robustness of the ENSO Signal}
\label{sec:res-robustness}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The preceding results leave RQ2 resting on a single number: the $3.3\%$
upper-tail improvement at the twelve-month horizon. The decisive question
is whether this gain accumulates steadily across the three decades of
ENSO cycles in the sample --- the signature of a structural climate
channel --- or materialises within a single episode. The first diagnostic
below answers this directly, and without any researcher-chosen break date;
it is therefore the primary test, and the same construction that allows it
to dispense with date choices is what makes its verdict robust to the
objectivation concerns that motivate the design. The two diagnostics that
follow --- lag structure and cross-commodity replication --- address
distinct generalisability concerns raised at the design stage and
corroborate the verdict from independent angles. Together the three test
three ways in which the full-sample gain could be sample-specific rather
than generalisable: temporal concentration in one episode, a lag structure
inconsistent with the proposed mechanism, and failure to replicate across
comparable commodities.

\subsubsection*{Temporal stability: expanding-window diagnostic}

To assess whether the ENSO gain is stable through time or instead driven by
a particular episode in the sample, we trace the \emph{cumulative} gain as
the evaluation horizon advances through time. For each calendar month~$T$ in
the out-of-sample period, define
\[
\Delta\text{QL}(T) \;=\;
  \text{QL}_{\text{HAR-X-QR}}\!\bigl(\{t \leq T\}\bigr)
  \;-\;
  \text{QL}_{\text{HAR-QR}}\!\bigl(\{t \leq T\}\bigr),
\]
where each loss is computed over all forecast--realisation pairs with
evaluation date up to and including~$T$. Negative values indicate that ENSO
augmentation has been beneficial on a cumulative basis up to that point. The
construction is fully recursive: no break date is assumed or imposed, and
the curve spans the complete out-of-sample window without any
ex-post partitioning.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/37_expanding_window_enso_matrix.pdf}
\caption{Expanding-window cumulative ENSO augmentation gain ($\Delta$QL)
  across horizons and quantile levels}
\label{fig:expanding-enso}
{\footnotesize Each panel shows $\Delta\text{QL}(T)$ over all out-of-sample pairs with evaluation date $\leq T$; negative values indicate cumulative net gain from ENSO augmentation. Rows: horizons (1, 3, 6, 12~months). Columns: $\tau \in \{0.50,\, 0.75,\, 0.95\}$.}
\end{figure}

Reading the curves requires keeping their construction in mind. Each is a
difference of two running means --- cumulative HAR-X-QR loss minus
cumulative HAR-QR loss, both averaged over every forecast--realisation
pair up to~$T$ --- so it is the \emph{slope}, not the level, that carries
the sign of ENSO's contribution in each subperiod. A downward-sloping
stretch is one in which augmentation reduces loss relative to baseline; a
flat stretch is one in which it has no net effect; and a near-vertical
step marks a single episode in which the two models diverge sharply. Two
construction artifacts must be read through. First, a running mean is most
sensitive to individual observations early in the sample, when few pairs
have accumulated, so the wide swings before roughly 2005 reflect the small
evaluation sample as much as the intensity of the late-1990s ENSO cycle.
Second, once an episode has displaced the cumulative mean, the curve drifts
back toward zero over the following years even when ENSO contributes
nothing further: that post-episode slope is dilution by later
near-neutral observations, not a reversal of sign. A structural channel
must therefore appear as a sustained downward drift across many years ---
not as a step followed by passive decay.

The figure is laid out as a matrix --- horizons down the rows, quantile
levels across the columns --- precisely so that the absence of a
structural signal can be read off directly. No panel in
Figure~\ref{fig:expanding-enso} exhibits the sustained-drift shape a
genuine climate channel would produce.
In every one of the twelve quantile--horizon combinations the cumulative
$\Delta$QL is either flat around zero or dominated by one or two abrupt
steps tied to identifiable episodes, with long stretches of no net
movement in between. The matrix as a whole therefore delivers the central
robustness finding before any single cell is examined: wherever ENSO
appears to help, the help arrives in discrete supply-stress episodes
rather than accruing across cycles. Two features of the layout sharpen
this reading. Moving down any column, the curves grow more step-like as
the horizon lengthens, because longer-horizon forecasts overlap a single
volatility episode many times and so register it as one large
displacement; moving across any row, the $\tau = 0.95$ column shows the
sharpest episodic steps, because the upper tail is where a volatility
spike registers most forcefully. The one cell that ends below zero --- the
twelve-month upper tail --- is thus also the cell in which a single
episode can most easily overturn three decades of accumulated sign.

Read on its own, that cell makes the point concrete. At the twelve-month
upper tail --- the only quantile--horizon combination with a material
full-sample gain --- the cumulative $\Delta$QL is \emph{positive} (ENSO
augmentation harmful on net) for essentially the entire evaluation period. An adverse
episode in the early 2000s pushes the curve sharply upward, after which it
declines only gradually as two decades of near-neutral observations dilute
the early loss. The single large favourable movement occurs in 2023--24,
when realised volatility spiked during the supply crisis while the ENSO
state was strongly El~Ni\~no; this final episode pulls the cumulative
curve just below zero, producing the full-sample endpoint of
$\Delta\text{QL} = -0.34 \times 10^{-4}$ (the $3.3\%$ gain). The six-month
panel tells the same story without the sign flip: its endpoint remains
marginally positive ($+0.07 \times 10^{-4}$), because the crisis episode
is not quite large enough to offset the accumulated deterioration.

The pre-crisis subsample makes the dependence explicit. Restricting the
evaluation to forecasts whose entire $h$-day target window precedes
February 2024 (for the twelve-month horizon, origins up to January 2023),
ENSO augmentation \emph{raises} the twelve-month upper-tail quantile loss
by 16\% ($\Delta\text{QL}_{\text{pre}} = +0.52 \times 10^{-4}$, on a
pre-crisis loss level of $0.000322$). The entire full-sample gain ---
and more --- is contributed by the 2023--24 window.

The other quantile levels reinforce the episodic reading. At the median
and $\tau = 0.75$, a favourable episode around the turn of the millennium
--- plausibly connected to the 1997--98 El~Ni\~no, the strongest on record
at that time --- produces a transient cumulative gain at the longer
horizons that dissipates over the following decade. Notably, this same
turn-of-the-millennium episode moves the central and upper-tail panels in
\emph{opposite} directions: a favourable downward dip at the median and
$\tau = 0.75$, but the sharp upward step that dominates the twelve-month
upper-tail panel. The same ENSO alignment that improved the central
forecast worsened the upper-tail forecast --- a sign reversal across
quantiles consistent with the discrimination--calibration tension analysed
in Section~\ref{sec:disc-coverage}. At $\tau = 0.75$ the
curve then turns and remains adverse, ending at
$+0.88 \times 10^{-4}$ at twelve months (the $-6.7\%$ entry in
Table~\ref{tab:har-x-improvement}); at the median the endpoint is
indistinguishable from zero. At no quantile--horizon combination does the
diagnostic show a steadily accumulating gain across successive ENSO
cycles: every favourable movement is confined to a specific supply-stress
episode, and the dominant one is the 2022--2024 crisis.

\subsubsection*{Two corroborating checks: lag structure and cross-commodity replication}

Two further checks, requested at the design stage when the apparent gain
was several times larger, confirm the temporal verdict from independent
angles; both are now reported compactly, with full tables in
Appendix~\ref{app:supplementary}. \emph{Lag structure:} if ENSO acted
through the agronomic-transmission chain (sea-surface anomaly $\to$ West
African rainfall $\to$ harvest shortfall $\to$ supply tightening $\to$
price volatility), predictive power should strengthen as the lag
approaches the 6--12 months over which that chain unfolds. The opposite
holds (Table~\ref{tab:lag-decay}): where ENSO helps at the twelve-month
upper tail it does so only contemporaneously (lag~0) or at lag~1, while
the agronomically motivated lags of three and six months are
\emph{worse} than the HAR-QR baseline with no ENSO term. \emph{Cross-commodity
replication:} a genuine Pacific climate channel should lift forecasts for
other tropical soft commodities with the same exposure. It does not
(Table~\ref{tab:cross-commodity}): on the identical HAR-X-QR specification,
ENSO \emph{worsens} twelve-month upper-tail forecasts for Arabica coffee
and produces only an economically negligible gain for raw sugar
($1.6\%$). The one commodity that shows any gain is the one whose sample
contains an idiosyncratic supply crisis.

\subsubsection*{Summary assessment of RQ2 robustness}

The diagnostics yield a consistent verdict, and the primary one settles it:
the full-sample twelve-month upper-tail gain of $3.3\%$ is the residual of
a cumulative deterioration spanning most of the evaluation period and a
single favourable episode in 2023--24, and in the pre-crisis subsample
ENSO augmentation makes the same forecast 16\% worse. The two corroborating
checks point the same way --- predictive power, where present, sits at
contemporaneous rather than agronomic lags, and the result does not
reproduce on coffee or sugar. The interpretation of this combined pattern,
and what it implies for the answer to RQ2, is taken up in
Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq2}.

\textit{First}, RQ1 receives a horizon-dependent answer. At short-to-medium
horizons the evidence for direct quantile regression is strong: HAR-QR
achieves the lowest upper-tail quantile loss from one day through three
months, and at one day and one week it is the top-ranked model in a Model
Confidence Set from which all three benchmarks are excluded
(Table~\ref{tab:mcs-ql95}). At long horizons the advantage disappears:
HAR-OLS overtakes HAR-QR at six months and GARCH(1,1) is the best
upper-tail forecaster at twelve months, 29\% below HAR-QR in quantile
loss. The long-horizon differences are not statistically significant ---
all models except historical volatility share $\mathcal{M}^{*}$ from three
months onward --- but the migration of the top MCS rank from HAR-QR (one
day to three months) to HAR-OLS (six months) to GARCH (twelve months)
consistently locates the value of explicit quantile estimation at the
short end. For mean forecasts, HAR-OLS dominates at every horizon and the
HAR family separates cleanly from GARCH under MSE and MAE
(Figure~\ref{fig:robustness-lineplot}).

\textit{Second}, RQ2 is answered in the negative. ENSO augmentation
worsens the upper-tail forecast at every horizon from one month to six
months and delivers only a modest $3.3\%$ improvement at twelve months
(Table~\ref{tab:har-x-improvement}). The robustness diagnostics in
Section~\ref{sec:res-robustness} show that this residual gain is
concentrated entirely in the 2023--2024 crisis window (pre-crisis, ENSO
makes the twelve-month forecast 16\% worse), follows a lag structure
inconsistent with the agronomic-transmission hypothesis, and does not
reproduce on comparable tropical commodities. The interpretation of this
combined pattern is developed in Section~\ref{sec:disc-rq2}.

\textit{Third}, coverage analysis reveals a systematic challenge at long
horizons. Both direct quantile regression models produce violation rates
far above the nominal 5\% at six months (11.45\% and 14.15\%) and twelve
months (14.63\% and 15.89\%), with the deterioration concentrated in the
crisis episode, during which realized volatility remained above the
$\tau = 0.95$ boundary for an extended period
(Figure~\ref{fig:upper-tail}, panel b2). ENSO augmentation worsens
calibration at every horizon from one month onward. The implications of
this calibration pattern are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc-coverage}.
